The book of Numbers opens with a date and a head count. The English name, Numbers, comes from the censuses in its first and twenty-sixth chapters. This unit is the first of the two. Keep the second in mind as you listen, because the book is built to be read from one to the other.

The date matters. Yahweh speaks to Moshe on the first day of the second month of the second year after the Exodus. The book of Exodus ended with the Dwelling raised on the first day of the first month and the cloud filling it so completely that Moshe could not enter. Leviticus then gave its laws without dating each speech. Numbers picks the clock up again a month after the raising, and later in the book the cloud will lift, on the twentieth of this same month. So this census is the last act of a long stay at Sinai. Everything in the first four chapters is preparation for leaving in order.

Notice where the speaking happens. The wilderness of Sinai is the place Israel reached in Exodus nineteen, and the phrase will bracket the whole stay. The speech now comes from the Tent of Meeting. Leviticus closed by saying its last laws were given on Mount Sinai, and Ramban asks why Numbers says the Tent again. Rashbam offers a different rule about when the mountain formula stops, and the unit's notes keep both.

Now the command itself. Yahweh tells Moshe to lift the head of the whole community. Levine takes the phrase to mean taking a poll, and the same idiom stood behind the shekel census of Exodus thirty. The men to be counted are those twenty and over who can go out to the army. The word for army, tsaba, is the same word Exodus used when it said Yahweh brought his armies out of Egypt. Levine makes this the key link of the opening chapters: the people who left as armies are now mustered as armies. Hold that in mind when the Levites stand outside the count.

Three words to watch. The first is paqad, which this study renders with account language, to take account of. Levine argues that its root sense is to attend to, or to assign, and that counting is only one of its uses. Ramban goes further and reads it as attention in every case. Both agree that this is more than adding up. The word will return in the sense of calling to account, and in this chapter it also means appoint, when the Levites are set over the Dwelling. The second word is community, which Levine reads as a social body assembled, not a congregation at worship. The third is the stranger, the one who comes near and dies. It stays unsettled whether that means any outsider, any Israelite who is not a Levite, or both.

Several tensions run under the surface. The total here, six hundred three thousand five hundred fifty, is exactly the figure Exodus gave for the half shekel ransom count six months earlier. Rashi, Ramban and Rabbeinu Bahya say this count too was taken through the shekels. Or HaChaim worries about the identical figure and answers it with the Levites. Ibn Ezra, in the lines we have, mentions no shekel in Numbers. Whitt and Levine read the chapter's composition through different models, though both treat the words you and Aharon in the third verse as an addition. Constable and Doty defend the numbers as literal thousands; Levine reads them as a tradition of large numbers whose significance has been exaggerated.

The chapter moves in four steps. First, the command and the naming of twelve chieftains, each with his father's name, and half of those names carry the divine names El or Shaddai. Second, the tribe by tribe tally, in a frame so uniform that it can be heard as a refrain; you will hear one tribe after another, in the order of the camps drawn in chapter two. Third, the sum. Fourth, the Levites, who are not counted but appointed, and the Dwelling at the centre with the tribes camped around it, so that wrath does not fall on the community.

Earlier passages this unit leans on, named and not retold: Exodus thirty and thirty eight on the shekel census, Exodus six and twelve on the armies of Yahweh, Genesis forty eight on Joseph's two sons standing in the place of Reuven and Shimon, Genesis forty nine on Reuven the firstborn, and Exodus forty on the raising of the Dwelling. Later the chapter returns in the second census, in the title of the called ones that the rebel chiefs will bear, and in the repeated warning that no one comes near.

Orient yourself, then, with a question. What does it mean for a people to be counted by the God who is about to live in the middle of them?
