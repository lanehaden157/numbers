The date and the place

Start with where the speech lands, because the book of Exodus left a door shut. When the Dwelling was raised on the first day of the first month, the cloud covered the Tent of Meeting, Yahweh's glory filled the Dwelling, and Moshe was not able to go in. Leviticus then spoke from that Tent. Numbers now opens a month later, on the first day of the second month of the second year, and it says Yahweh spoke to Moshe in the wilderness of Sinai, in the Tent of Meeting. Levine reads the date as part of the Priestly writers' habit of dating events like an official chronicle, with the same formula returning at the ninth, tenth and twentieth chapters and in the list of journeys. The date does a second job. The cloud will lift on the twentieth day of this month, and both Ibn Ezra and Rashbam say the census and the arrangement of the camp had to come before the departure. So the census is a piece of travel planning.

The phrase wilderness of Sinai is itself a clue. It first appears when the people arrive at Sinai in the nineteenth chapter of Exodus, and Levine says all the events from there to the lifting of the cloud in Numbers ten belong to it. The wording stays when the setting changes from mountain to Tent. Rashbam draws a rule from that: once the Dwelling stood, the mountain formula gives way to the Tent formula. Ramban knows that Leviticus still says Mount Sinai in its twenty-fifth chapter, after the raising, and answers that Numbers repeats the Tent here because that earlier speech was out of step with the rest. The two do not quite answer each other. Levine adds a different layer: his note hears two ideas of the Tent, a centre of worship in the Priestly writers and an oracle tent outside the camp in the older story of Exodus thirty-three, and says they should not be blended. Whitt reads the same oddities as a sign of a long composition history; he notes that the opening speaks of their departure with no antecedent, which he takes to show that Numbers once ran on from Exodus without a break. Treat that as his inference.

The command

Yahweh says to lift the head of the whole community. Levine reads the idiom as taking a poll, and it already stood in Exodus thirty, where Yahweh orders a count of everyone twenty and over, each paying half a shekel as a ransom for his life so that no plague falls when they are counted. Ibn Ezra treats the two as the same idiom. The men to count are those twenty and over, all who go out to the army. The word for army is the same word that Exodus used when Yahweh brought his armies out of Egypt, and Levine builds his reading of these chapters on it: the exodus generation is a fighting force on the march. Notice how Rabbeinu Bahya reads the same Exodus line otherwise, taking the armies as angels who went out with Israel. That is reception, not what the Hebrew of Numbers says.

The verb that carries the chapter is paqad. Levine says its basic sense is to hand over or assign, so to turn attention to someone, and that counting follows from mustering. For him the noun pequddim is not usually "totals." Ramban goes the other way and sees attention and supervision in every use, then draws a strong consequence: Moshe was not to count heads, he was to supervise the half shekels, and that is how he knew the number. Rashi on the second verse says by their polls means by the shekels, a beka a head, as in Exodus thirty-eight. Look at what the text itself offers: the phrases by the number of names and by their heads, and no shekel. Ibn Ezra's lines on these verses, as we have them, do not mention the shekel. This is a real split, and the notes keep it open.

Three smaller words. Community, the ordinary term for Israel gathered, is for Levine a social word, tied to a root meaning to meet, and he sets it beside the Ugaritic assembly of the gods and the Jewish colony at Elephantine. Clan and patriarchal house, as Levine renders the next pair, are flexible; he argues the house sits between the tribe and the clan. And the age line, twenty, is not the only one in the Torah. The Levites serve from thirty to fifty in the fourth chapter and from twenty-five in the eighth. Levine suggests the difference between eligibility and service, with years of training between. That is his harmonising, not something the text says. In the third verse you and Aharon looks to Levine like an explanatory gloss, and Whitt agrees that it was added, though he puts it in a fifth stage of the book's growth. The singular verb that closes the section, he mustered them, fits Whitt's view that Moshe alone was the original counter. The seventeenth verse and the forty-fourth say Moshe and Aharon together.

The twelve

Twelve heads of houses are to stand with Moshe, one from each tribe, and the text names them with their fathers. Most of the names, Levine says, occur only in the early chapters of Numbers. Nachshon son of Amminadab, chieftain of Yehudah, is the notable exception, and the name has a long afterlife: Exodus six makes Aharon's wife Nachshon's sister, Ruth four puts him in David's line, and Matthew one carries him into the genealogy of Jesus. Half the twenty-four names, by my count, carry El or Shaddai. Levine adds that several of these names turn up in Ammonite name lists of the seventh century, and calls the list more traditional than historical. Doty hears Egyptian Ra in Ahira; Levine reads ra as friend.

Yoseph's sons take two places so that the number stays twelve once Levi is set aside. Levine ties this to Jacob's words in Genesis forty-eight, where Ephrayim and Menashsheh become his as Reuven and Shimon are. Ibn Ezra and Bahya each explain the order in the list: the sons of Rachel before her servant's, Ephrayim before Menashsheh as Jacob blessed them, Dan first among the servants' sons. Note that the list puts Gad eleventh, while the later tally puts him third. The tally follows the camps that chapter two will draw, starting with Reuven's.

The sixteenth verse gives the twelve a title, the called ones of the community. In the form Levine follows, that is the same wording that comes back in the sixteenth chapter, where two hundred fifty chiefs rise against Moshe as called of the assembly, and in the twenty-sixth, where Datan and Abiram are called of the community and then rebel with Qorach. Rashi says these are the men called for every weighty matter; Ibn Ezra, that the community does nothing unless it is called. Hear the irony coming.

Carrying it out

Moshe and Aharon take the named men and gather the whole community on that same first day. Ramban notices the zeal of that, and says the count began that day and was not finished that day, which is why the nineteenth verse repeats it was done in the wilderness of Sinai. Pulpit says the natural sense is a one-day census, and that the family lists were ready beforehand. The verb for they registered their descent is a form found only here; Levine compares a later phrase in Ezra two. Rashi says they brought records and witnesses. Ramban rejects the documents and says each man declared his family and tribe as he came with his shekel, and notes that the twenty-sixth chapter does not use this verb at all. Ibn Ezra says they gave their birth dates, to prove they were twenty. Sforno gives the purpose: for war the army should be genealogically sound.

The tally

Now twelve entries in one frame, with the tribes in the order of the camps. The first, Reuven, is the only one to open with the narrative verb there were, and the first two entries are the only ones that spell out by their heads. Bahya says the phrase appears only for these two because their founders' sins needed atonement; that is a homily, and the reason is his own. Reuven is called firstborn of Yisrael, the title Exodus six gave him and Genesis forty-nine took away. Sforno, noting the title here, says Reuven kept his place in heaven's books because he repented.

The Masoretic text puts a paragraph break after each tribe. The figures are large. Judah is greatest, Menashsheh least, and the total, six hundred three thousand five hundred fifty, is the same as the figure of Exodus thirty-eight. Constable notes that every figure ends in zero and takes that as a sign the numbers were rounded. Doty answers Butzer and Gray at length, saying the text never claims the people lived off the land, and he sets aside Gray's arithmetic from the number of firstborn, which belongs to the third unit. Levine reads the totals as a tradition of great numbers and says the word eleph here means one thousand, not clan. Whether they are literal, Levine's field call and Doty's defence both stand.

Then the question the shekels raise. Or HaChaim asks how the same total could hold after six months of growth, and answers that the Levites were inside the earlier count and are outside this one. Henry and Pulpit offer versions of the same arithmetic. Rashi is silent on this unit's totals.

The Levites

Verse forty-seven says the Levites were not counted, and the forty-eighth begins a new speech: the Levites are not to be counted among the people but appointed over the Dwelling of the Testimony. Here is the wordplay Levine catches. The verb that has run through the chapter, to take account of, is the verb for what is not done to the Levites, and the same root in the causative is what is done to them: you are to put them in charge. In Greek the pun is lost, because the verb for put in charge is a different word from the one in the third verse.

Why are they exempt? Ibn Ezra and Rashbam say it was because they were never military; their charge was the Dwelling. Ramban says Moshe left them out on his own because no chieftain had been named for Levi, and Yahweh then told him he was to count them apart. Sforno says they were waiting for instruction. Bahya says the exemption marks superior rank, not unfitness, and with a midrash links it to their loyalty at the calf; Doty makes the same link with caution.

What do they do? They carry the Dwelling and its vessels, take it down when it sets out and set it up when it camps, and the one who comes near who is a stranger is put to death. Whitt notes this puts all the handling on the Levites, and that later material, which he dates to a later stage, gives the first handling to Aharon and his sons; the fourth chapter has Aharon and his sons take down the veil and cover the holy things. Rashi: the stranger is put to death by heaven; Ibn Ezra: by a court. Levine reads stranger as unconsecrated Israelites and non-Israelites alike, and chapter eighteen supports a wide reading, since there the Israelites themselves may not come near the Tent lest they bear sin and die.

The fifty-second verse puts the tribes each in its own camp under its own company, and the next puts the Levites around the Dwelling so that wrath will not come on the community. Wrath here is Levine's danger of divine anger, and the clause is echoed in the same words in chapter eighteen. Ibn Ezra says it means no one from Israel is struck, as with Uzzah. Rashi says there will be wrath if outsiders share the Levites' work, as at Qorach. Whitt reads defilement of the shrine causing Yahweh's anger.

The last clause says the Levites keep the charge of the Dwelling. Levine argues the word means a duty to keep, not guard duty; he disagrees with Milgrom's earlier reading. Ramban and Ibn Ezra take it as guarding, and Ramban says it was a night patrol, and a model for the later watches at Jerusalem. The Greek supports the guard reading, with a word for guard.

The close

The unit ends with the line that closed the building of the Dwelling in Exodus: the Israelites did as Yahweh had commanded Moshe, so they did. Levine calls it almost proverbial. Here it answers the first order of the book, and the people who have just been numbered are, for a moment, entirely obedient. Hold that obedience against the twenty-sixth chapter, which says that of those counted in Sinai no one remains but Caleb and Yehoshua.
